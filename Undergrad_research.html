Getting Started in Undergraduate Research

Interested in science but not sure what working in a research lab actually involves—or how to get started? This informal guide is for undergraduate students who want to explore research, gain practical experience, and learn more about possible careers in science.

The guide was originally developed by my friend and colleague Dr. Dylan Catlett, and I’ve adapted it based on my own experiences working with undergraduate researchers.

There isn’t one correct path into research, and the advice here is just a starting point. I encourage you to talk with professors, teaching assistants, graduate students, and other researchers to learn about different opportunities and experiences.

Career Paths in Science

A background in science can lead to many different careers, including research and teaching at universities and community colleges, government agencies, environmental consulting, policy, law, education, and the private sector.

Undergraduate research is a great way to explore these possibilities while developing skills that are useful across many careers: teamwork, critical thinking, communication, problem-solving, and collecting, analyzing, and interpreting data.

You don’t necessarily need a graduate degree (MS or PhD) to build a fulfilling career in science. However, graduate education can open additional opportunities, particularly for careers involving independent research.

Unlike undergraduate education, research-focused graduate programs generally place greater emphasis on developing and completing original research projects. Prior research experience can strengthen your application, but students enter graduate school through many different pathways.

Many research-based graduate programs offer financial support through research assistantships, teaching assistantships, or fellowships. These may cover tuition and provide a stipend and health insurance, although funding, compensation, and benefits vary considerably among programs and institutions. It’s important to investigate these details before accepting an offer.

Before You Start

You don’t need to be a genius or have a perfect GPA. Curiosity, persistence, willingness to learn, and enthusiasm are much more important than already knowing everything about a subject.

You don’t need to know exactly what you want to study. Research can help you discover what interests you. Taking classes, attending departmental seminars, and talking with researchers are great ways to explore different fields.

Think about what you’d like to gain from the experience. Are you interested in laboratory techniques such as microscopy, culturing, genomics, or spectroscopy? Would you prefer computational research, fieldwork, or a combination? Are you hoping to complete a senior thesis or simply explore what research is like?

Consider the time commitment. Research becomes more rewarding as you gain experience, and many labs prefer students who can participate for at least a semester or two. Summer research programs may offer shorter, more intensive opportunities. However, getting involved in research is an opportunity to explore your interests—not a commitment to a particular career or laboratory.

Expect to learn the fundamentals. Undergraduate researchers often begin with tasks such as preparing materials, washing glassware, organizing samples, or entering data. These are important parts of scientific research, but they should also provide opportunities to learn why the work matters, develop new skills, and gradually take on more responsibility.

Ask about compensation. Both paid and volunteer research opportunities exist, as do programs that provide scholarships, stipends, or academic credit. Whenever possible, seek opportunities that recognize and support the time you contribute.

Remember that research takes many forms. Some labs focus on experiments, others on field observations, computational analyses, or theoretical work. Ask what a typical day or week looks like before committing to a position.

Research can be slow and that’s normal. Experiments fail, equipment breaks, analyses take longer than expected, and some days involve repetitive tasks. Use quieter periods to read scientific papers, ask questions, explore data, and think about the bigger picture.

Steps to Get Involved in a Research Lab

Prepare a résumé or curriculum vitae (CV)

At this stage, a one-page résumé or CV is usually sufficient. Don’t worry if you haven’t had a research position before!

Consider including your education, relevant coursework, employment or volunteer experience, technical and computer skills, and other activities that demonstrate responsibility, collaboration, or initiative.

You can find useful guidance through ASU’s Career Services.

Identify research groups and opportunities

Explore faculty profiles on departmental websites in subjects that interest you. If a professor’s research sounds interesting, visit their laboratory website to learn more about their projects, methods, and research group.

As you explore, keep track of researchers whose work interests you, their contact information, and any instructions for prospective undergraduate assistants.

You can also look for structured undergraduate research programs, scholarships, and summer research opportunities. Some programs require a faculty mentor, so identifying potential research groups early can be helpful.

Apply or reach out

Some research programs and laboratories have formal application processes. Read their instructions carefully and make sure your application addresses what they’re looking for.

For other labs, there may not be an advertised position or application process. In that case, it’s perfectly acceptable to contact a professor, graduate student, or postdoctoral researcher directly.

This is often called cold-emailing, and it’s a common way for students to find their first research opportunity.

Cold-emailing potential mentors

Contacting someone you’ve never met can feel intimidating, but researchers are generally accustomed to receiving inquiries from students.

Start by identifying several research groups that genuinely interest you. Your email doesn’t need to be long, but it should introduce you, explain what interests you about their work, and ask whether they have opportunities for undergraduate researchers.

Personalizing your message makes a difference. You don’t need to understand every detail of a professor’s research, but demonstrating that you’ve explored their work helps communicate your interest.

Not everyone will respond, and some labs won’t have space or funding for additional students. Don’t interpret a lack of response as a reflection of your abilities. If you haven’t heard back after a week or two, it’s reasonable to send one polite follow-up.

Graduate students and postdocs can also be excellent people to contact, particularly because they often work closely with undergraduate researchers.

Here’s an example of an email that Hannah Baetge, an undergraduate studying Marine Science and Conservation at Duke University, might send to a professor.

Dear Dr. X,

My name is Hannah, and I’m a second-year undergraduate student majoring in Marine Science and Conservation at Duke. I’m writing to ask whether you have any opportunities for undergraduate students to get involved in your research.

I’ve been interested in marine microbiology since taking MARSCI 201, The Future of the Ocean. I came across your lab website and enjoyed learning about your work on coastal phytoplankton and bacteria. I was particularly interested in your research combining laboratory experiments and satellite observations to investigate how wildfire ash affects plankton communities.

Although I’m still exploring my research interests, I’d be excited to learn more about this work and contribute to your lab.

I’ve attached my résumé and would be happy to provide any additional information.

Thank you very much for your time and consideration.

Best regards,

Hannah Baetge

Example: A follow-up email

If you haven’t received a response after a week or two, you can reply to your original message with a brief reminder.

Dear Dr. X,

I wanted to follow up on my previous email about potential undergraduate research opportunities in your lab.

I’m still very interested in your research and would appreciate the opportunity to learn whether you might have space for an undergraduate assistant.

I’ve reattached my résumé for convenience. Thank you again for your time!

Best regards,

Hannah

Example: Responding to an invitation to meet

Suppose the professor responds:

Hi Hannah,

Thanks for your interest in our research! I’ve just returned from fieldwork with my students, so apologies for the delayed response. Would you be available to meet sometime in the next few weeks?

Best,

Dr. X

Hannah could reply:

Dear Dr. X,

Thank you for your response! I’d love to meet and learn more about your research.

I’m available on Tuesday or Thursday afternoons next week, or Monday morning the following week. Please let me know if any of those times work for you.

Thanks again, and I look forward to speaking with you!

Hannah

Preparing for a Meeting or Interview

If a researcher invites you to meet, congratulations! This is an opportunity for both of you to learn whether the research group would be a good fit.

Learn about the research. Explore the lab website and, if possible, read one or two of the group’s scientific papers. You don’t need to understand everything. Take notes about what interests you and questions you’d like to ask.

Prepare questions about the experience. For example, what would your responsibilities be? How much time would you be expected to contribute? Who would supervise and train you? Are paid positions, scholarships, or academic credit available? Could the experience eventually lead to an independent project or senior thesis?

Be professional, but be yourself. Arrive on time, dress comfortably and appropriately for the setting, and be honest about what you know and what you’d like to learn. Asking questions is a strength, not a weakness.

Remember that you’re evaluating the lab, too. A good research experience should provide a respectful environment, clear expectations, opportunities to develop skills, and someone you can approach with questions or concerns. It’s completely reasonable to ask how undergraduate students are mentored and supported.

Follow up afterward. Thank the researcher for their time and, if you’ve agreed on any next steps, follow through.

A Final Thought

Your first research experience doesn’t have to define the rest of your career. You might discover a field you love, realize that a particular type of research isn’t for you, or find new interests you hadn’t considered before.

All of those outcomes are valuable.

The important thing is to stay curious, ask questions, and remember that everyone starts somewhere.
